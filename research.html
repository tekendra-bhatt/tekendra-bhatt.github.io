Research Overview

How do past events change the chance of future events? This question motivates my research in applied probability, stochastic processes, and statistical inference.

I study Hawkes processes, which describe event sequences in which an event can trigger further activity. My work combines mathematical theory, simulation, and estimation to understand these patterns and develop methods for analyzing event data.

  • Hawkes processes and self-exciting point processes.
  • Oscillatory and nonmonotonic excitation kernels.
  • Stability, ergodicity, and asymptotic theory.
  • Maximum likelihood estimation and Fisher information.
  • Analytic moments and differential operator methods.
  • Simulation and reproducible statistical computing in R.

My current manuscripts and software projects are described on the Publications page.

Why Oscillatory Hawkes Processes?

In a Hawkes process, each event contributes to the rate of future events through an excitation kernel. A common choice is an exponentially decaying kernel, which models an influence that fades steadily with time.

Some systems exhibit delayed rebounds or recurring peaks in activity. I investigate sinusoidally modulated, exponentially decaying kernels that allow an event's influence to rise and fall while gradually fading. Under suitable parameter restrictions, these kernels remain nonnegative and preserve the branching interpretation of a linear Hawkes process.

The oscillations describe variations in excitation. They do not represent inhibition below the background event rate. Distinguishing these mechanisms is important when interpreting a fitted model.

A more flexible kernel also creates statistical challenges. My work examines when parameters can be identified, how reliably they can be estimated, and when the additional structure improves the description of data.

Current Research

Oscillatory Models and Their Foundations

I develop Hawkes models with oscillatory excitation kernels and study conditions for stability, ergodicity, and identifiability. The classical exponential model appears as a special case, supporting comparisons between simpler and more flexible descriptions of event clustering.

Likelihood Inference and Uncertainty

I study maximum likelihood estimation for parametric Hawkes processes. Under appropriate assumptions, my analysis establishes consistency and asymptotic normality of the estimators, accounting for the relationship between stationary models and observations over finite time intervals.

For oscillatory models, I investigate Fisher information and develop analytic expressions and approximations to support uncertainty quantification. Simulation studies help assess estimation accuracy and the practical behavior of these approximations.

Analytic Moments through Differential Operators

A second research direction develops a systematic approach to moment calculations for linear Hawkes processes. For kernels admitting a suitable finite-order differential representation, the method reduces associated moment equations to ordinary differential equations.

I investigate exponential, gamma-type, and oscillatory kernels within this framework. The goal is to clarify how kernel parameters influence event-count variability and provide useful tools for further statistical analysis.

Computation and Model Assessment

I implement simulation, likelihood evaluation, optimization, and diagnostic tools in R. My computational work emphasizes reproducible experiments and clear comparisons between models.

Applications to financial event data examine how oscillatory kernels describe temporal patterns. Broader interests include event data from neuroscience, cybersecurity, and other settings where activity clusters over time.

Student Research Opportunities

Simulation provides an accessible starting point for studying stochastic processes. Students can begin with a simple model, generate event sequences, and investigate how changing assumptions affects the resulting patterns.

Possible Undergraduate Projects

  • Simulate Poisson and Hawkes processes and compare their event counts, waiting times, and clustering.
  • Explore how excitation strength and decay affect simulated event sequences.
  • Study how observation length influences parameter estimation accuracy.
  • Compare empirical moments from simulations with theoretical predictions.

These are potential projects that can be adapted to a student's preparation. They provide opportunities to develop computational skills, explain mathematical findings, and produce a reproducible report.

Proposed Master's Capstone: When Events Breed Events

I presented this proposed capstone project to master's students at Augusta University. It focuses on modeling clustered event data and forecasting event-count probabilities with Hawkes processes.

Potential application tracks include neural spike trains and cybersecurity event logs. The proposed sequence covers simulation, data preparation, maximum likelihood estimation, comparison with a Poisson baseline, and evaluation on held-out observations.

The project is intended to culminate in reproducible code, a written report, and a presentation. Students interested in pursuing it are welcome to contact me to discuss preparation and scope.

Future Directions

My research agenda builds on the connection between probabilistic foundations, statistical methods, and computational evaluation. Directions I plan to pursue include:

  • Diagnostics and forecast evaluation: Develop methods for assessing model fit, checking uncertainty estimates, and comparing predictions.
  • Multivariate and network models: Extend oscillatory excitation to interacting event streams and study their stability and identifiability.
  • Robust statistical inference: Investigate finite-sample behavior and the effects of model misspecification.
  • Flexible kernel models: Explore marked and nonparametric extensions while maintaining interpretable model structure.
  • Research software: Develop documented R tools for simulation, estimation, and diagnostics, with examples suitable for researchers and students.

I welcome conversations about applied probability, statistical modeling of event data, and collaborations that connect mathematical research with student learning.
